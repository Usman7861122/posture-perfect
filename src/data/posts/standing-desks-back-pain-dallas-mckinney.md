A standing desk can be a useful way to change positions during a long workday. But if you trade eight hours of sitting for eight hours of standing, you may simply trade one kind of discomfort for another. The aim is not to stand as long as possible. It is to make it easier to move throughout the day and build the capacity to do your work comfortably.

Whether you commute along the Dallas North Tollway, work from home in McKinney or spend your day in a North Texas office, you can start with a few changes that do not require expensive equipment. Here is what research tells us about sit-stand desks, how to set one up and when recurring symptoms deserve a closer look.

## Do standing desks actually help back pain?

An adjustable desk gives you the choice to work seated or standing. It may help you spend less time sitting, but that does not automatically mean it treats back pain.

A [2018 Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012487_workplace-interventions-increasing-standing-or-walking-decreasing-musculoskeletal-symptoms-sedentary) found that sit-stand desks reduced sitting time in the short and medium term, although confidence in the evidence was low and longer-term effects were uncertain. A 2019 Cochrane review examined workers with musculoskeletal symptoms and found insufficient evidence to conclude that interventions increasing standing or walking reliably relieved their pain.

A 2020 systematic review likewise found no clear advantage for prolonged standing over prolonged sitting in reported low back pain. A desk can make changing positions easier; it is not a medical treatment or a guarantee of relief.

## Why standing all day is not the answer

Both sitting and standing require your body to tolerate a position over time. When you sit for a long stretch, you may feel stiff when you first get up. When you stand for a long stretch, you may notice tired feet, aching legs or back discomfort. Your response can depend on your existing symptoms, footwear, fitness, work demands and health conditions.

A 2024 observational study of more than 83,000 UK adults found that standing time was not associated with a lower risk of major cardiovascular disease, and greater standing time was associated with a higher risk of certain circulatory conditions. The study cannot establish cause and effect or determine an ideal standing duration for an individual. It does reinforce an important distinction: standing still is not the same as being physically active.

You do not need to fear sitting, nor should you force yourself to stand through pain. A workstation should support several comfortable positions, not lock you into one of them.

## How to set up a sit-stand workstation

Use these practical checks in both positions. They are adapted from the [OSHA computer workstation guidance](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations).

- **Screen:** Place your main monitor directly in front of you at a height that allows a comfortable forward gaze. Adjust text size rather than leaning in to read. If you use bifocals, a lower screen may be more comfortable.
- **Keyboard and mouse:** Keep them close enough that your shoulders can relax and your elbows remain near your sides. Adjust the desk height so your wrists do not have to bend sharply while typing.
- **Chair:** When sitting, support your feet and choose a backrest that feels comfortable. Allow room behind your knees and change the backrest angle occasionally.
- **Standing position:** Wear comfortable footwear, distribute weight naturally and avoid holding yourself rigidly in a military-style posture. Shift your weight or briefly support one foot on a low, stable footrest if comfortable.
- **Laptop:** If you raise a laptop screen, use a separate keyboard and mouse so your hands are not forced upward to reach the keys.

You should not have to maintain a precise angle all day. A comfortable setup is one you can use without excessive reaching or straining, with the freedom to change positions whenever you need to.

## How long should you stand at a standing desk?

There is no proven universal ratio of sitting to standing that eliminates pain. If you are new to a standing desk, experiment with a small amount of standing rather than beginning with several uninterrupted hours.

Here is a sample starting routine for an adult who can safely stand and walk. It is a flexible example, not a medically validated prescription:

- Work seated for about 25–30 minutes.
- Stand for 5–10 minutes while answering email or reading, if comfortable.
- Take a short walk or change tasks for 2–5 minutes before returning to a comfortable position.
- Repeat as your schedule and symptoms allow. If standing makes symptoms worse, shorten the interval or sit back down.

On meeting-heavy days, build your breaks around existing tasks: walk to get water, stand during a brief call or take a lap around the office between appointments. OSHA’s work-process guidance encourages brief pauses during computer work. A reminder can help, but you need not follow the clock rigidly.

If you have dizziness when standing, balance difficulties, a recent operation, significant leg swelling or an existing condition that limits standing, ask your healthcare professional about a suitable routine. Seated movement and supported position changes may be more appropriate.

## Three simple ways to add movement to your day

For adults without an acute injury or concerning symptoms, these are examples of gentle activity breaks. Start with a comfortable range and stop if an exercise causes worsening pain, dizziness, numbness or radiating symptoms.

**1. A short walk.** Leave the desk for a few minutes at an easy pace. At home, walk around a room; at work, use a safe hallway or outdoor path. If walking is limited, use an accessible movement option suited to your abilities.

**2. Supported sit-to-stand.** From a sturdy chair that will not roll, stand up slowly, then sit back down with control. Try a few repetitions if comfortable. Use the armrests for support if needed. Skip this exercise if it is painful or unsafe for you.

**3. Gentle shoulder movement.** Relax your arms, slowly roll your shoulders a few times, and then let them settle. Avoid forcefully pulling your shoulders back or pushing your neck into an uncomfortable position.

Short movement breaks are useful habits, but they are not a replacement for a personalized exercise program. The [CDC adult activity guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) recommends building toward at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week and strengthening on at least two days, adapted to your abilities. Even smaller amounts are a reasonable starting point.

## Do I need a better desk or a stronger body?

You may benefit from both, but they address different needs. An adjustable desk changes the demands of a task. Exercise can help you work toward tolerating daily demands with greater strength, mobility and endurance.

An October 28, 2026 Cochrane review found that workplace ergonomic adaptations alone may have little meaningful effect on outcomes for adults with work-related neck, shoulder or arm complaints. This is not a review of low back pain specifically, and it does not mean workstation adjustments are useless. It means changing equipment alone may not address a person’s symptoms.

A separate September 2026 systematic review of exercise for office workers with chronic low back pain found possible short-term pain improvement, but the evidence was very uncertain. These findings favor an individualized approach rather than promises about a particular desk or exercise.

## When desk-related pain needs evaluation

Recurring pain is not necessarily evidence that your posture has damaged your spine. It can have many contributing factors, including joint or muscle irritation, nerve involvement and other medical conditions. An evaluation can help distinguish these possibilities.

Arrange a healthcare evaluation when symptoms are not improving, regularly interrupt work or sleep, or travel into your arm or leg. Persistent numbness, tingling or weakness warrants prompt assessment. Do not assume every symptom is caused by your desk.

Seek emergency medical care for new bowel or bladder control problems, numbness around the groin or saddle area, rapidly worsening weakness, severe spine pain after a significant injury, or back pain accompanied by fever. These symptoms should not wait for a routine clinic appointment. See Mayo Clinic’s [back pain guidance](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/back-pain/symptoms-causes/syc-20369906) and [neck pain guidance](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/neck-pain/symptoms-causes/syc-20375581).
